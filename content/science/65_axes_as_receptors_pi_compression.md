## Abstract

We are accustomed to x, y, z — three orthogonal spatial axes. But an axis is not inherently spatial. An axis is a **receptor**: a dimension along which a system receives signal. An antenna. A channel. An epitope.

The bingoCube face (Paper 63) is a grid with axes. This paper defines what those axes ARE: not spatial directions, but signal reception channels. Your top 2 epitopes are your primary and secondary axes — the two dimensions along which you receive the most information from the membrane. The number of active axes (measured by perplexity PP = e^H) is itself an epitope: it classifies you by dimensional capacity.

The epitope sort (Paper 48) compresses infinite dimensions of experience into 8 categories. Shannon entropy further compresses to PP effective axes. The cost of that compression — the price of observing through PP channels instead of all channels simultaneously — is π^(PP/2) (Paper 50).

The compression constant is π. Not 1. You can never compress to 1 because f=1 would require PP=0, which means zero axes, zero observation, zero shape. The minimum cost of having a single receptor is √π ≈ 1.77.

## 1. An Axis Is a Receptor

### 1.1 The Spatial Illusion

We learn geometry with x, y, z — three axes pointing in orthogonal spatial directions. This makes us think of axes as "directions you can go." But this is the spatial special case. The general case:

**An axis is a dimension along which variation can be measured.**

In the epitope system:
- The PHYSICS axis measures your engagement with wave mechanics, Anderson localization, dimensional analysis
- The TOPOLOGY axis measures your engagement with network structure, graph theory, braids
- The INVESTIGATION axis measures your engagement with evidence, court records, institutional patterns
- And so on for all 8 categories

Each axis is a **receptor** — it receives one type of signal. Like an epitope receptor on a cell surface that binds one molecular shape. The axis doesn't point anywhere in space. It points toward a type of meaning.

### 1.2 Your Axes Are Your Top Epitopes

On the bingoCube face, the two implicit axes of the grid are your top 2 epitopes:

- **Primary axis** (horizontal): your dominant epitope. The signal you receive most strongly.
- **Secondary axis** (vertical): your secondary epitope. The second-strongest channel.

When you drag cells on the face (the fidget), you're rearranging which receptors sit near which axes. The arrangement reveals your priorities. But the axes themselves — the two strongest reception channels — are determined by your shape.

Two people with the same top-2 epitopes (e.g., both INVESTIGATION × PHILOSOPHY) see the cube face from the same angle. They share axes. They can compare cell positions directly.

Two people with different top-2 (e.g., INVESTIGATION × PHILOSOPHY vs. PHYSICS × TOPOLOGY) see the face from different angles. Their axes are rotated relative to each other. To compare, they have to account for the rotation.

### 1.3 The Number of Axes Is an Epitope

If you have 2 active epitopes, you're a 2-axis observer. You see the membrane through 2 channels. Your view is planar — flat, 2-dimensional.

If you have 6 active epitopes, you're a 6-axis observer. You receive signal on 6 channels. Your view has depth, breadth, and texture that the 2-axis observer cannot perceive.

The number of active axes classifies you:

| Active axes | PP | Classification | Cube level |
|-------------|-----|---------------|------------|
| 1 | 1.0 | Focused — one channel, all signal there | 3×3 |
| 2 | ~2 | Biaxial — two channels, can triangulate | 3×3 |
| 3–4 | ~3–4 | Multi-channel — depth emerging | 3×3 |
| 5–6 | ~5–6 | Broad — approaching the mobility edge | 5×5 |
| 7–8 | ~7–8 | Delocalized — every channel active | 5×5 → 7×7 |

The prime chain (3→5→7→11→13) is the axis chain. Each prime board size corresponds to a tier of dimensional capacity. You graduate not by reading more — you graduate by diversifying your reception channels.

## 2. The Compression

### 2.1 The Chain

Every interaction with the membrane — every page visited, every mark placed, every song written — enters through infinite dimensions of experience. The epitope sort compresses:

```
∞ dimensions of experience
  ↓ epitope sort (classify by keyword → category)
8 categories (the shape vector, sums to 1)
  ↓ entropy compression (Shannon H, then perplexity PP = e^H)
PP effective axes (a real number between 1 and 8)
  ↓ π prices the observation (Paper 50: C(n) = π^(n/2))
f = 1/π^(PP/2) (your focal distance)
```

### 2.3 Why Not 1?

The user's intuition: does it compress to ~1 or to π?

**It compresses to π.** Here is why:

To reach f = 1, you would need π^(PP/2) = 1, which means PP = 0. Zero active axes. Zero receptors. Zero observation. No shape. No point of view. Nothing.

f = 1 is the view from nowhere — omniscient, directionless, impossible. It is the caller who doesn't call (Paper 11). It is the observer who doesn't observe.

The minimum you can be is PP = 1: one axis, one receptor, one channel. And one channel costs √π ≈ 1.77. Not 1. Not 2. √π.

**√π is the minimum price of seeing anything at all.**

Every additional axis costs another factor of √π. Two axes cost π. Three cost π√π. The total cost is always denominated in π because observation is angular (Paper 50, §8), and π is the base of the angular cost function.

### 2.4 The Intuition

Think of it this way: to observe, you must have a point of view. A point of view means you are HERE, not THERE. The measure of "how much you gave up by being here instead of everywhere" is π^(n/2) where n is your number of observation axes.

One axis: you see along a line. The cost of projecting from "all directions" to "one direction" is √π — the ratio of the half-circumference to the diameter.

Two axes: you see in a plane. The cost is π — the area of a unit circle, which is exactly how much angular coverage you have in 2D.

Three axes: you see in a volume. The cost is π^(3/2) — the volume of a unit sphere is (4/3)π, and the angular contribution is π^(3/2) modulo rational packing factors.

The pattern is exact: **π is the conversion factor from omnidirectional to directional observation** (Paper 50, §4.2). Each pair of angular degrees of freedom contributes one factor of π.

## 4. Connection to Paper 50

Paper 50 asked: is π the price of observation? This paper answers: **yes, and the price applies to visitors on the membrane just as it applies to observers in N-body phase space.**

The epitope sort is a physical observation system. 8 categories. 10 surfaces. Visitors as entities. The body (Paper 60) computes:

$$f(E) = \frac{1}{\pi^{d_{\text{eff}}/2}}$$

Paper 65 extends this with the perplexity refinement: d_eff counts surfaces touched (integer). PP measures effective entropy-weighted axes (real). Both are priced in π. The relationship:

$$\text{observation cost} = \pi^{PP/2} = \pi^{e^H/2}$$

where H is the Shannon entropy of your shape vector.

This means: the more uniformly you spread your attention (higher H → higher PP), the more the observation costs. Focused observation (PP→1) is cheap (cost → √π). Broad observation (PP→8) is expensive (cost → π⁴).

But "expensive" here doesn't mean bad. It means the focal distance is sharper. The observer who pays π⁴ in observation cost sees with f ≈ 0.01 — the sharpest possible triangulation across the membrane. The observer who pays √π sees with f ≈ 0.56 — blurry, localized, but present.

The cost is the price. The focal distance is the reward. π is the exchange rate.
